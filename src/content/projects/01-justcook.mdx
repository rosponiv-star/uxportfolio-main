<Chapter title="Context">

JustCook started as a university project at the University of Trento and grew across three courses — semiotics of visual representation, sociology of communication and psychology of communication — between September 2024 and May 2025. We were a team of four students, and I worked across every phase: research, synthesis, service concept, interface design, packaging and campaign.

The brief was open: pick a real problem in our own community and design a response to it. We chose the one we were living every day as off-site students in Rovereto: eating well when you are away from home for the first time. The project is framed by two UN Sustainable Development Goals, good health (SDG 3) and responsible consumption (SDG 12).

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="The problem">

<Note label="Reframe" text="The question changed from “how do we teach students to eat healthily?” to “how do we make the healthy option the easiest one to execute?”">

Desk research painted a familiar picture. More than 60% of off-site students eat take-away at least once a week, only 44% eat a portion of fruit a day, and 92% change their eating habits after moving out. Knowledge was not the issue: students know what a balanced meal looks like.

What gets in the way is structural. Time is scarce and unpredictable, budgets are tight, cooking confidence is low and planning a week of meals is one more cognitive load on top of studying. The problem was never motivation. It was the effort between intention and plate.

</Note>

> How might we make a balanced, home-cooked meal the path of least resistance for students who are short on time, money and kitchen space?

</Chapter>

I wanted to see the problem where it happens, so the research moved into students’ apartments. A closed-question survey, shared in student WhatsApp groups and through QR flyers in study rooms, gave us the broad picture. Semi-structured interviews, held in the participants’ own kitchens and supported by photo elicitation, explained the why behind it. Participant observation covered the three moments that shape a meal: the grocery run, the cooking and the way fridge and pantry are organised.

Every interview and field note was coded in a thematic grid of 8 themes and 36 codes, from autonomy and weekly planning to budget, shared living and food waste. The grid turned four conversations and three observations into patterns we could compare and defend.

</Chapter>

To decide what the service had to deliver, I mapped the students’ jobs, pains and gains in a value proposition canvas and prioritised them. The must-haves were clear: a balanced diet, low cost and time saved. Variety from the same ingredients, a predictable execution time and clear recipes came right after.

<Chapter title="Design">

JustCook is a service with two layers that work together. The app reduces the decisions; the box reduces the effort.

### The box: mise en place, delivered

Each box holds four or five fresh, pre-dosed ingredients for a single portion, and every box unlocks up to ten different recipes, so the same base never means the same dinner. Everything is ready in about twenty minutes. Four boxes cover the week with different nutritional profiles: A is high-protein, B is energising, C is light and D is comfort food.

Boxes are reserved in the app and collected at a partner supermarket. A code inside each box registers it, unlocks its recipes and updates the user’s pantry.

### The app: a planner that bends

<Note label="Decision" text="The plan adapts to the week, not the other way round. Students mark the days they’re away or overloaded, and the app simply plans around them.">

**Adaptive weekly planning.** Students assign boxes to the days they will actually cook. The app checks the plan against what they own and gives a binary answer: the week is covered, or some boxes are missing, with one tap to buy or scan them.

**A nudge at the right time.** Reminders are timed backwards from the recipe’s duration and the user’s dinner time, before decision fatigue and delivery apps win. Cooking for tomorrow’s lunchbox moves the reminder to the evening before.

**Focus mode.** Recipes play as a vertical, step-by-step video feed, a pattern students already know from social media, and only show what can be cooked with the box or the ingredients at hand.

**Streaks with friends.** A weekly score and a small leaderboard with flatmates reward showing up, which is what turns cooking into a habit, without judging what is on the plate.

</Note>

### Brand and launch

The name is a wink at Just Eat. Where delivery asks you to do nothing, JustCook asks you to do just one thing, and makes it easy. The tone is reassuring and empowering: cooking for yourself is presented as a small act of independence, not a chore. The campaign used the channels where students already are: Instagram, WhatsApp groups and QR flyers in study rooms. The flyers show the same maze twice, the long way around without JustCook and straight through with it.

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Outcome" tone="alt">

The project delivered a complete service concept: a behavioural research report, a high-fidelity Figma prototype of the app, packaging for four boxes, and the brand and campaign to launch them.

We translated the goals into targets that a pilot would have to meet:

<Chapter title="Reflection">

JustCook taught me that the most useful design decision can be a reframing. The project only moved when we stopped trying to change what students knew and started changing what was easy for them to do.

Doing it again, I would invest more in the evidence. I would run more interviews and a more structured analysis, test the prototype with real students before polishing it, and build the targets from data rather than ambition. The concept is strong; the next version would be a validated one.

</Chapter>
