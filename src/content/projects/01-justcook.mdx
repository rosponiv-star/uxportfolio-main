<Chapter title="Challenge" summary="Effort, not knowledge">

<Key>Students know what a balanced meal looks like. What stops them is the effort between intention and plate.</Key>

A university project at the University of Trento, across three courses (semiotics, sociology and psychology of communication). A team of four; I worked on every phase, from research to packaging.

The brief: pick a real problem in our own community. We chose the one we were living ourselves as off-site students, and desk research confirmed it.

<BigStat value="92%" label="of off-site students change their eating habits after moving out." source="Lupi et al., 2015 (258 students)" />

<Note label="Reframe" text="From “how do we teach students to eat healthily?” to “how do we make the healthy option the easiest one?”">

<Chapter title="Reflection" summary="What I’d validate next">

<Key>The most useful design decision was a reframing: changing what was easy to do, not what students knew.</Key>

What surprised me most was how little the problem had to do with knowledge. Every student we spoke to knew what a balanced meal looks like. They were busy, tired and cooking in a kitchen shared with flatmates. Sitting in those kitchens taught me more than any statistic.

It was also my own problem. I was one of those off-site students, and designing for people like me made it easy to assume. The research kept correcting me, and I learned to let it.

If I started again tomorrow, I would:

1. Talk to more students, beyond the four in-kitchen interviews.
2. Analyse what they said in a more structured way.
3. Put the prototype in students’ hands before polishing it.
4. Build the targets from data rather than ambition.

<Closing kicker="What I take with me" line="Design the easy path, and people will take it.">

JustCook is still a concept, on paper and in Figma. **The next version would be a tested one.**

</Closing>
