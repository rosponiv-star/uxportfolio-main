<Chapter title="Challenge" summary="Effort, not knowledge">

<Key>Students know what a balanced meal looks like. What stops them is the effort between intention and plate.</Key>

A university project at the University of Trento, across three courses (semiotics, sociology and psychology of communication). A team of four; I worked on every phase, from research to packaging.

The brief: pick a real problem in our own community. We chose the one we lived as off-site students: more than 60% eat take-away weekly, only 44% eat fruit daily, and 92% change their eating habits after moving out.

<Note label="Reframe" text="From “how do we teach students to eat healthily?” to “how do we make the healthy option the easiest one?”">

> How might we make a balanced, home-cooked meal the path of least resistance for students short on time, money and kitchen space?

</Note>

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Research" summary="Inside students’ kitchens">

<Key>I moved the research into students’ kitchens, where the problem actually happens.</Key>

A survey, shared in student WhatsApp groups and through QR flyers, gave the broad picture. Interviews in participants’ kitchens, with photo elicitation, explained the why. Observation covered the three moments that shape a meal: shopping, cooking and storing.

Everything was coded in a thematic grid of 8 themes and 36 codes, so four conversations became patterns we could compare and defend.

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Design" summary="Box, app and launch">

<Key>Two layers that work together: the app reduces the decisions, the box reduces the effort.</Key>

### The box

Four or five fresh, pre-dosed ingredients for one portion, ready in about twenty minutes. Each box unlocks up to ten recipes, so the same base never means the same dinner. Four profiles cover the week: protein, energy, light and comfort. Boxes are reserved in the app and collected at Conad; a code inside registers each one and updates the pantry.

### The app

<Note label="Decision" text="The plan adapts to the week, not the other way round: students mark the days they’re away and the app plans around them.">

**Adaptive planning.** Boxes go on the days students will actually cook; the app says whether the week is covered.

**A timely nudge.** Reminders are timed backwards from the recipe’s duration and dinner time, before a delivery app wins.

**Focus mode.** Recipes play as a vertical, step-by-step video feed, a pattern students already know.

**Streaks with friends.** A weekly score with flatmates rewards showing up, not what is on the plate.

</Note>

### Brand and launch

The name winks at Just Eat: delivery asks you to do nothing, JustCook asks for one easy thing. The campaign runs where students already are: Instagram, WhatsApp groups and QR flyers showing the same maze twice, the long way and the JustCook way.

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Reflection" summary="What I’d validate next">

<Key>The most useful design decision was a reframing: changing what was easy to do, not what students knew.</Key>

Next time I would invest more in evidence: more interviews, a more structured analysis, usability tests with real students before polishing, and targets built from data rather than ambition.

</Chapter>
