<Chapter title="Challenge" summary="Woken at the wrong moment">

<Key>An alarm does one thing: it breaks your sleep at a fixed time, whatever state you are in.</Key>

Designed for Human–Computer Interaction 1, a course at the University of Trento: one of my first design projects.

A team of two. I led the concept and designed the app.

### Who it is for

People who can’t wake up rested, above all those with demanding hours: busy professionals and students.

<Note label="Reframe" text="From “a more pleasant ringtone” to “a room that wakes you gradually, at the moment your sleep is lightest.”" />

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Define" summary="An alarm with no buttons">

<Key>The alarm has no buttons and no screen. Everything is set from the phone, and the wearable decides when to act.</Key>

The wearable does three jobs: it finds a light sleep phase to wake you in, it knows when you are awake, and it records how you slept.

</Note>

### Built to last

No screen, a recycled aluminium body and a wooden shell. Gradual light also uses less energy than a lamp switched on at full power.

<Chapter title="Reflection" summary="What I’d research first">

<Key>AuraWake was strong on the system and thin on evidence: I designed the answer before checking the question.</Key>

### What I learned

Thinking in systems came naturally: the phone, the wearable and the room each have a clear job. What was missing was anyone who actually struggles to wake up.

### If I started again

1. Start from research: sleep diaries and interviews with students and people working shifts.
2. Test the core idea on its own: does a gradual, well-timed wake-up feel better than an ordinary alarm?
3. Weigh the cost: four devices are a lot to buy and set up for a better morning.

<Closing kicker="What I take with me" line="Research first, then the system.">

AuraWake is an early concept, designed in Figma. **It taught me to ask who it is for before deciding what it does.**

</Closing>

</Chapter>
