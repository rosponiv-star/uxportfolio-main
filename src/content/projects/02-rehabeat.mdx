<Chapter title="Challenge" summary="Recovery without a coach">

<Key>Recovering from a sports injury at home means exercising alone, with nobody to say whether a movement is right.</Key>

The brief, for a human–computer interaction course at the University of Trento: help amateur athletes return to their sport after an injury, safely and without making it worse.

A team of four over one semester. I worked on every phase, from research to the app and TV interfaces.

### The evidence

<BigStat value="65%" label="of athletes return to their pre-injury level of sport after ACL reconstruction." source="Ardern et al., 2014 (69 studies, 7,556 participants)" />

The same review found that a positive psychological response favours the return: recovery happens in the head as much as in the knee.

<Metrics
  items={[
    { value: '30–50%', label: 'Don’t keep up their home exercise programme', text: 'Musculoskeletal patients (Argent et al., 2018).' },
    { value: '139', label: 'Studies link music to better mood and lower perceived exertion', text: 'Meta-analysis, 3,599 participants (Terry et al., 2020).' },
  ]}
/>

### The question

<Note label="Reframe" text="From “how do we send exercises home?” to “how do we replace the guesswork with evidence, and the silence with rhythm?”">

> How might we give injured amateur athletes the eye of a physiotherapist at home, and a reason to keep going?

</Note>

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Define" summary="One target, two archetypes">

<Key>Two archetypes drop out for opposite reasons: one is afraid of moving wrong, the other is bored of moving at all.</Key>

### The target

Amateur athletes aged 18 to 50, recovering at home from a sports injury. Motivated to get back to their sport, busy with everything else, and keen on fewer visits and trips to a clinic.

<Note label="Decision" text="Adults up to 50. Younger athletes need a guardian and closer clinical follow-up; past 50, other conditions and the risk of falls call for a specialist’s eye. Inside that range, the plan still starts from a doctor’s report." />

<Chapter title="Reflection" summary="What I’d validate next">

<Key>The brief looked technical. The real problem was emotional: fear on one side, boredom on the other.</Key>

### What I learned

Sensors and AI were the easy part to imagine. What decided the design was confidence: a system that shows the athlete a movement is right, instead of just asking for it.

The music idea taught me to treat an emotional need as a functional one. Rhythm is not decoration here: it is the instruction.

### If I started again

1. Put the prototype in front of injured athletes before refining the screens.
2. Bring in a physiotherapist from the first week, to check the exercises, the safety limits and what the AI may change.
3. Test the music idea on its own, with a quick experiment on whether matching the beat really improves form and adherence.

<Closing kicker="What I take with me" line="People keep going when they can see they’re doing it right.">

Rehabeat is still a concept, designed in Figma. **Its next step is a session with a real athlete and a real physiotherapist.**

</Closing>
