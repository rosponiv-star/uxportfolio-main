<Chapter title="Challenge" summary="Comfort that brings worry">

<Key>A smart home is meant to bring peace of mind. Often it brings new doubts: is the oven off, who rang, who still has a key.</Key>

The brief, for a graphical interface design course at the University of Trento: design the software for a company that builds and installs home automation hardware.

A team of three over one semester. We shared every phase equally, from domain modelling to the Figma prototype.

### The brief

<Note label="Constraint" text="The app manages devices already installed. Selling, installing and servicing hardware stay with the partner company.">

The partner’s goal: one app for every device in the house, so its customers feel in control and come back for more devices.

</Note>

<Chapter title="Ideate" summary="Sketch alone, merge together">

<Key>Each of us sketched alone first, so the ideas could diverge before we merged them.</Key>

Concepts were drawn by hand, on paper, one pain point and one persona at a time.

We then laid them out in a shared design space, grouped by pain point, and checked that no idea worked against another persona.

<Note label="Method" text="For each pain point we kept the smallest set of elements that solves it; everything else became secondary. Features were ranked, not piled up." />

### Checked against the models

Once the flows existed in Figma, we ran them against the original sequence models: wherever the app replaced a touchpoint, the experience had to get shorter.

At the door, for example, answering the call opens a control panel on the phone, instead of a walk to the intercom.

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Design" summary="Plan, door, codes, alarm">

<Key>One rule across the app: show the state of the house first, and make every risky action hard to trigger by accident.</Key>

### The structure

Five sections, always one tap away in the navigation: home, security, access, automations, history. Each room keeps its own colour everywhere, on the floor plan and on the device cards.

### Safety first

<Note label="Trade-off" text="Material Design doesn’t intend the slider for confirming actions. We used it anyway: a deliberate, continuous gesture that can’t fire by accident, with no dialog covering the live video.">

**Opening the gate takes a slide, not a tap.** The action lets someone into the house, so it asks for a gesture nobody makes by mistake.

</Note>

<Note label="Decision" text="Pressing Cancel by mistake costs nothing. Setting off, or switching off, the alarm in a hurry does.">

**Arming and disarming ask for confirmation, and Cancel gets the emphasis.** The safe answer is the easy one to hit.

</Note>

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Reflection" summary="What I’d test next">

<Key>A smart home earns trust by showing its state, not by adding more controls.</Key>

### What I learned

The most useful feature turned out to be the least technical: a clear answer to “is everything all right at home?”

Designing for delegates, couriers and children taught me that a home app has users who never open it.

### If I started again

1. Interview real households, including an older person and a babysitter, before building the personas.
2. Test the riskiest flows with people: opening the gate from afar, arming the alarm with a window open.
3. Take on the problems left out, starting with calling for help in an emergency.

<Closing kicker="What I take with me" line="Calm is a feature: design the state before the controls.">

Smart Home Hub is a concept, prototyped in Figma. **Its next step is a test in a real home, with the people who share it.**

</Closing>

</Chapter>
